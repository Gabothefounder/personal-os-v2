## Central Question

Qu’est-ce qui crée de la confiance?

What creates trust?

Not: how to extract more trust. Not: how to appear trustworthy. The question is what actually produces justified trust, when trust is the wrong instrument, and what lets people cooperate when they cannot fully know or trust each other.

---

## Why I Am Exploring This

**GABRIEL**

The world is moving fast — technology, geopolitics, institutions. It is getting harder to know who and what deserves trust: people, companies, media, governments, AI.

ScanScam started from a concrete version of this: scams, impersonation, messages that look legitimate. Families and ordinary people have to decide, quickly, whether something is real. That is not a branding problem. It is a trust-calibration problem in daily life.

The same difficulty appears at larger scales. When you delegate power — to a person, an institution, a platform, an AI agent — you need some reason to believe their actions will stay aligned with your values, or at least not quietly work against them. Alignment is not automatic just because something is competent or official.

There is also trust in ourselves and in the future. If you stop believing that effort, building, and cooperation can still work, you stop starting things. That matters for businesses, capital, and whether people are willing to work across borders with people they do not already know.

AI agents are starting to receive authority: to recommend, to transact, to speak on someone’s behalf. The old shortcuts — a professional-looking email, a confident voice, a familiar logo, a fluent answer — are getting cheaper to fake. So the question is not only “can I trust this person?” It is “what should I trust, on what evidence, and how much vulnerability is justified?”

I am interested in trust brokering: mechanisms that let parties cooperate even when they do not fully know or trust each other. That might be a product, a practice, infrastructure, or something else. It is not yet a company claim.

This thesis exists to keep those questions honest, instead of collapsing them into a slogan.

## Core Distinctions

Do not collapse these into one word.

**Trust**
A stance of the truster: willingness to accept vulnerability under uncertainty, based on an expectation about another’s intentions or behaviour. Trust is granted. It is not a property of the other side.

**Trustworthiness**
Attributes of the trustee (person, institution, system, agent) that would justify trust. Someone can be trustworthy and still not be trusted. Someone can be trusted without being trustworthy.

**Reliance**
Depending on someone or something in practice. Reliance can come from trust, but also from contracts, lock-in, habit, lack of alternatives, or force. Using a system is not proof that you trust it.

**Confidence**
Expectation that things will go as assumed — often toward systems, institutions, or the future — sometimes without a felt choice to be vulnerable. You can have confidence in a process and still not trust a person inside it.

**Legitimacy**
Perceived right to exercise authority or make claims. People may comply with a legitimate institution without personally trusting its occupants. Legitimacy is not the same as competence or alignment.

**Competence**
Ability to perform in a relevant domain. Necessary for trustworthiness in that domain. Not sufficient. A competent actor can still be misaligned, predatory, or outside the domain you actually care about.

**Alignment**
Whether the other party’s interests, values, or incentives point in a compatible direction. Close to “benevolence” in the classic trustworthiness triad (ability / integrity / benevolence), but includes institutional and machine alignment, not only personal goodwill.

**Verification**
Obtaining evidence that reduces uncertainty. Verification can substitute for trust, complement trust, or come before trust. It is not trust. A verified claim can still leave you exposed.

## Research-Supported Findings

**RESEARCH** unless marked otherwise.

- Trust generally involves accepting vulnerability under uncertainty. If there is no vulnerability, “trust” is usually just prediction or indifference.
- Trust and trustworthiness are not the same thing. Conflating them hides both gullibility and unearned suspicion.
- Ability/competence, integrity, and benevolence/alignment are recurring dimensions of perceived trustworthiness. People often judge these separately, and a failure in one can dominate the others.
- Initial trust can rely heavily on similarity, appearance, reputation, and social proximity. These are cheap first-pass signals.
- These signals do not necessarily predict actual trustworthiness. They predict whether trust is granted, not whether it should be.
- Repeated behaviour and reciprocity provide stronger evidence over time than first impressions, credentials, or narrative.
- Trust can reduce transaction friction and facilitate cooperation, trade, investment, and innovation. Causality is complex: trust may enable exchange, successful exchange may produce trust, and third variables (institutions, wealth, culture, enforcement) may drive both.
- Institutions, law, contracts, escrow, certification, reputation systems, and verification can reduce how much personal trust is required. Cooperation often scales by substituting mechanisms for intimacy.
- High trust is not always desirable. Functional distrust, checks and balances, second opinions, and limits on authority can be valuable. Blind trust is a failure mode.
- Trust signals can be counterfeit, borrowed, correlated, or irrelevant to the domain being evaluated. A trusted brand in one domain does not automatically transfer.
- AI makes many surface trust signals cheaper to manufacture: writing, images, voices, identities, apparent expertise, and social proof.
- **SYNTHESIS:** Provenance, independently verifiable history, accountability, and costly-to-fake behaviour may therefore become increasingly important relative to appearance and fluency.
- People are increasingly using AI-mediated information while often reporting relatively low trust in those channels. Use and trust are diverging in some settings. This looks more like reliance under convenience than a vote of confidence.
- Different languages and information environments can cause the same AI system to produce materially different outputs. Causes may include data availability, alignment differences, application layers, and technical artifacts — not necessarily a coherent cultural worldview.

Contradictory / limiting notes (preserve):

- Survey measures of “trust in X” are noisy, culturally loaded, and often mix confidence, approval, competence, and legitimacy.
- High-trust rhetoric can coexist with high fraud, weak institutions, or low interpersonal trust in daily life.
- Some low-trust environments still transact at scale through enforcement, collateral, and repeated dealing.

## Working Hypotheses

These are **HYPOTHESIS**, not findings. They should be tested, narrowed, or killed.

### H1

We may be entering a trust-calibration problem rather than simply a trust crisis.

People still trust, rely, and delegate. The harder problem may be matching the amount and kind of trust to the evidence, domain, and exposure — especially when signals that used to be informative are now cheap to fake.

### H2

As easily manufactured trust signals become cheaper, authenticated history, provenance, accountability, and costly-to-fake signals may become more valuable.

If fluency, polish, and social proof inflate, the scarce resource is evidence that is expensive to counterfeit and attached to an accountable party.

### H3

Trust brokering may become increasingly valuable where uncertainty, information asymmetry, or vulnerability prevents otherwise valuable cooperation.

The opportunity, if it exists, is not “make people feel safer.” It is unlocking transactions, relationships, or coordination that currently do not happen because justified trust is missing or too expensive to establish.

### H4

The most valuable trust intermediaries may not create personal trust; they may structure interactions so less personal trust is required.

Escrow, verification, staged exposure, and recourse can produce cooperation without the parties liking or knowing each other. Personal warmth may be a side effect, not the product.

### H5

AI agents will require graduated trust and authority rather than a binary trusted/untrusted status.

Competence in drafting is not authority to send money. Alignment on tone is not permission to represent you. Authority should be earned, scoped, revocable, and domain-specific.

### H6

Trust may increasingly become economic infrastructure when it expands the radius of people, companies, machines, or countries able to cooperate.

If true, the economic question is not the feeling of trust. It is whether more valuable exchanges become possible, cheaper, or faster.

### H7

Loss of confidence in the future or in institutions may reduce entrepreneurship, capital deployment, and willingness to build. This must be distinguished from taxes, regulation, returns, demographics, and other material explanations.

Do not treat “people don’t trust the future” as the default explanation for low investment or low company formation.

## Failure Modes of Trust

Ways trust (or the appearance of trust) goes wrong:

- **Counterfeit signals** — manufactured fluency, fake identity, fake reviews, fake credentials, synthetic media.
- **Borrowed prestige** — using a trusted institution, person, brand, or aesthetic as a halo for an unrelated claim.
- **Correlated evidence** — many “independent” signals that actually come from one source or one incentive.
- **Domain mismatch** — trusting someone in the domain where they were proven, for a decision in a domain where they were not.
- **Compromised verifier** — the auditor, platform, certificate, or “source of truth” is captured, lazy, or itself unverified.
- **Misaligned incentives** — the trusted party gains by being believed more than by being right or loyal.
- **Overestimated competence** — integrity and warmth hide inability; confidence hides gaps.
- **Exposure greater than evidence justifies** — the size of the vulnerability is larger than the quality of the proof.
- **Excessive trust** — skipping checks that would be cheap relative to the downside.
- **False certainty produced by AI synthesis** — a coherent answer that compresses uncertainty, buries disagreement, and sounds like a finding.

## Mechanisms That Enable Cooperation

These can support, substitute for, or ration trust. They are not interchangeable.

| Mechanism | What it does |
|---|---|
| Identity | Who is acting, persistently enough to attach history |
| History | What they have done before, in relevant conditions |
| Reputation | What others report, with whatever bias that channel has |
| Networks | Who will vouch, introduce, or sanction |
| Verification | Evidence checked against a claim |
| Provenance | Where a claim, file, or artifact came from |
| Contracts | Explicit terms and conditions of the exchange |
| Collateral | Skin in the game that can be lost |
| Escrow | Third-party holding of value until conditions are met |
| Insurance | Transfer of residual risk |
| Certification | Standardized attestation by a named party |
| Rules / law | Background constraints and public enforcement |
| Accountability | Named party who can be blamed, sued, fired, or excluded |
| Recourse | What you can do after harm |
| Shared incentives | Reasons the other side wins if you win |
| Paced vulnerability | Small exposure first; more only after evidence |
| Repeated interaction | Future dealings that make defection more expensive |

**SYNTHESIS:** Cooperation often scales by stacking several of these so that personal trust can stay small.

## Trust Brokering

**HYPOTHESIS / working definition**

A trust broker is an intermediary that makes cooperation possible between actors who cannot rely solely on personal familiarity or complete information.

Possible mechanisms (not a product spec):

- verify claims
- translate between parties
- establish shared facts
- reduce information asymmetry
- structure exposure
- provide guarantees or recourse
- transfer credible reputation
- establish shared rules
- protect vulnerable participants
- surface alignment and conflicts
- pace increases in vulnerability

Historical / modern analogues: merchant networks, banking, escrow, certification, regulators, auditors, marketplaces, diplomacy, NGOs, professional intermediaries.

**Not a finding:** “Trust Broker” is not yet a validated standalone profession or business opportunity. The analogues show that the function exists. They do not show that Gabriel should occupy it, that customers would pay for a new category with that name, or that ScanScam is already that company.

## ScanScam Connection

**HYPOTHESIS — current positioning, not a committed claim**

“ScanScam helps you know what deserves your trust.”

Possible progression to test, not a product roadmap:

message → claim → company → counterparty → deal → AI recommendation → trust/risk intelligence

Each step is a different object of evaluation, a different vulnerability, and probably a different buyer. Do not treat the ladder as inevitable. ScanScam currently lives nearest the first rungs (suspicious messages / scam risk). Moving right requires evidence that people will use and pay for help at that layer, not a more ambitious sentence.

## Open Questions

**OPEN**

- Is there actually a broad decline in trust, or are different kinds of trust moving differently (interpersonal vs institutional vs media vs AI vs future-confidence)?
- What variables predict actual trustworthiness, as opposed to perceived trustworthiness?
- When does verification substitute for trust, and when does it only postpone the moment of vulnerability?
- How much economic value can a trust intermediary capture, versus merely creating value that others appropriate?
- Where is insufficient justified trust actually preventing valuable transactions?
- How will AI agents authenticate counterparties and evidence?
- What should remain human judgment even when agents are competent?
- How should agents earn greater authority — by history, scoped permissions, human countersignature, insurance, or something else?
- Does Canada / Québec possess a measurable trust advantage, and in which dimensions?
- Is confidence in Québec’s entrepreneurial future actually declining, or is that a mood, a media story, or a material-conditions story?
- How does trust affect capital deployment once returns, regulation, liquidity, and demographics are accounted for?
- Which trust mechanisms remain hard to counterfeit in an AI-rich environment?

## Counter-Thesis

Reasons the whole Trust Thesis may be overstated:

- Material incentives may explain behaviour better than trust. People cooperate when it pays, defect when it pays, and talk about trust afterward.
- Good institutions may create trust rather than the reverse. If so, “build trust” is the wrong lever; build enforcement, predictability, and recourse.
- Distrust can be adaptive. In scam-rich, propaganda-rich, or high-agency-cost environments, suspicion is not a pathology.
- Markets frequently function through enforcement, collateral, and repeat dealing rather than interpersonal trust.
- “Trust” risks becoming an umbrella explanation for unrelated phenomena: legitimacy crises, inequality, media fragmentation, low returns, housing, demographics, AI product quality.
- Survey trust measures have major methodological and cultural limitations. Cross-country and time-series comparisons can mislead.
- AI problems may be data, evaluation, product, and engineering problems rather than trust problems. Users may not “need to trust AI”; they may need it to be checkable, scoped, and liable.

If the counter-thesis is mostly right, the useful work is still real (scam protection, verification, provenance, scoped agent authority) but should not be framed as a grand theory of trust.
